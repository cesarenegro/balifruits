## 01 — Coconut / *Cocos nucifera*

Template: **A**  
Left count: **779**  
Right count: **1755**  
Note count: **126**

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Coconut is one of Bali’s most useful coastal plants, visible wherever village gardens, temple grounds and sandy lowlands meet the sea. A single palm can give drinking water, milk, oil, grated flesh, fuel, fibre and ritual material, while its tall crown marks the horizon like a living landmark. Young coconuts are opened for their cool water; mature ones enter sauces, sweets and daily cooking. The husk becomes coir, the shell becomes charcoal or small utensils, and the young leaves are folded into offerings. Around Bali, coconut is not only a fruit but a practical companion to household life, ceremony and the ecology of shorelines. It belongs to the kitchen, the market, the beach path and the temple gate, carrying usefulness from root to crown. Few plants feel essential.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

The coconut palm, Cocos nucifera, grows with a slender ringed trunk and a high crown of long arching fronds. Its fruits develop in clusters below the leaves, changing from green and heavy with sweet water to brown, fibrous and oil-rich as they mature. In Bali, this sequence is understood through use: kelapa muda is valued as a refreshing drink, while older coconuts are grated, squeezed and cooked into santan, the coconut milk that gives depth to sauces, sweets and ceremonial dishes. The palm is also woven into ritual life. Young coconut leaves, or janur, are folded, cut and plaited for canang sari, penjor decoration and many forms of temple offering; older leaves and midribs can become temporary screens, brooms or simple household material. The husk supplies fibre, the shell can be burned as charcoal, and the trunk may be used in modest construction where suitable timber is needed. Coconut oil is used in cooking, body care and traditional household preparations, while grated coconut appears in spice pastes and sweets. Ecologically, coconut palms tolerate salt spray, heat and sandy soils better than many fruit trees, so they belong naturally to Bali’s lowland and coastal edges. They cast broken shade, catch wind, and help bind loose ground, though true coastal protection depends on intact dunes, mangroves and other vegetation as well. The plant’s power is its completeness: water inside the fruit, oil inside the flesh, fibre around the shell, and a crown of leaves that moves constantly in the sea air. Few plants show so clearly how a botanical form can become food, craft, landscape and ceremony at once. In Bali, a coconut palm is rarely just scenery; it is a working presence beside the house, the shrine, the road and the shore.

### NOTE ECOLOGICHE

Coconut gives water, oil, fibre and ritual leaves, while its palms mark and help stabilize Bali’s sandy coastal lowland edges.

### CARD

- **Season:** Year-round
- **Location:** Coastal areas and lowlands
- **Tree height:** 20–30 m
- **Yield:** 50–100 coconuts per year
- **Main characteristics:** Hydrating water, oil, fibre and ritual leaves

## 02 — Banana / *Musa spp.*

Template: **A**  
Left count: **774**  
Right count: **1755**  
Note count: **128**

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Banana is one of the quiet constants of Bali’s cultivated landscape. It grows beside houses, in mixed gardens, along irrigation edges and around village compounds, sending up broad leaves that catch light and tear naturally in the wind. The plant is not a tree but a giant herb: each pseudostem fruits once, then gives way to new shoots from the base. Its fruit is eaten fresh, steamed, fried or cooked into sweets, while the leaves are as important as the bananas themselves, wrapping food, lining trays and carrying offerings. In Bali, banana is everyday nourishment, kitchen material and garden architecture at the same time. A clump can contain fruit, shade, young shoots and fallen leaves returning moisture and fibre to the soil. It is renewable and tied to daily use.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

The banana plant, Musa spp., rises from an underground corm and forms a false trunk made of tightly wrapped leaf sheaths. This explains its rhythm: fast, generous and renewable. After months of leaf growth, a large hanging inflorescence appears, protected by purple bracts; the female flowers become the familiar hands of fruit, while the male bud may continue below. Around Bali, different bananas are used in different ways. Sweet dessert bananas are eaten ripe, smaller types are offered in ceremonial trays, and firmer cooking bananas may be steamed, fried or prepared in snacks. The leaves are equally central. They are used as natural plates, parcel wrappers and cooking envelopes for preparations such as pepes and tum, where steam and heat release a soft green aroma into the food. In markets and kitchens they still give a biodegradable, elegant alternative to plastic or paper. The plant also belongs to household ecology: old leaves fall back as organic matter, pseudostems hold moisture, and clumps protect small soil patches in mixed gardens. Because bananas sprout continuously, a family compound can contain several generations of the same plant at once: young shoots, bearing stems and cut stumps returning nutrients to the ground. The flower bud, leaf, fruit and trunk all have practical value, but the plant never feels industrial; it remains close to the human scale. To observe banana in Bali is to see a domestic form of abundance: soft shade, food within reach, and leaves large enough to become both plate and offering. Its torn leaves are not a defect but a response to wind, making the plant flexible in open tropical weather. This is why the banana reads as both plant and infrastructure, quietly serving food, ceremony and soil.

### NOTE ECOLOGICHE

Banana offers fruit, cooking leaves and organic mulch; its renewing clumps fit naturally into Bali’s home gardens and wet edges.

### CARD

- **Season:** Year-round
- **Location:** Gardens, compounds and cultivated plots
- **Tree height:** 2–7 m herbaceous plant
- **Yield:** 1 bunch per pseudostem
- **Main characteristics:** Fruit, cooking leaves and renewing shoots

## 03 — Mango / *Mangifera indica*

Template: **B**  
Left count: **417**  
Right count: **1779**  
Note count: **130**

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Mango trees give Bali shade before they give fruit. Their dense crowns stand in house compounds, village lanes and small orchards, carrying young copper leaves, fragrant panicles and later the heavy green shapes of the fruit. Eaten sour with salt or ripe and golden, mango moves easily between snack, juice, dessert and seasonal market pleasure. In Bali it is remembered as much for shade as for sweet seasonal fruit.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

Mangifera indica is a long-lived tropical tree with a broad canopy, resin-scented leaves and large flower panicles that appear before fruit set. In Bali, mango is familiar in both rural and domestic landscapes: it is planted for shade, for fruit, and for the pleasure of waiting through the dry season until the first ripe fruits appear in markets. The fruit is used at several stages. Young green mango is cut and eaten with salt, chili or palm-sugar dressings, giving acidity and crunch; ripe mango becomes soft, perfumed and sweet, eaten fresh, blended into juice, served with sticky rice-style desserts, or added to fruit plates. The leaves are not a common everyday food in Bali, but mango foliage has a long history in tropical household medicine and in ritual decoration in parts of South and Southeast Asia; here it is safer to read the Balinese tree mainly as fruit, shade and seasonal presence. Ecologically, the mango is valuable because its canopy cools yards, shelters birds and insects, and drops organic matter beneath the tree. Flowering attracts small pollinators, while fallen fruit feeds animals and enriches the soil when not collected. A mature mango tree also changes the character of a compound: children remember climbing it, families wait for its crop, and market sellers sort fruits by ripeness and aroma. It is not a delicate plant in appearance; it is generous, solid and social. Its best quality is perhaps this mixture of patience and sudden abundance. For months the tree is shade and leaf; then, almost all at once, it becomes fragrance, colour and sticky fingers. In a Bali fruit chapter, mango should feel less like an exotic object and more like a seasonal household companion rooted in everyday life. It closes the garden with shade and scent.

### NOTE ECOLOGICHE

Mango gives shade, insect flowers and seasonal fruit, turning Bali house gardens into cool, productive social spaces and memories.

### CARD

- **Season:** October–February
- **Location:** Lowlands, village gardens and compounds
- **Tree height:** 10–30 m
- **Yield:** 100–300 fruits per mature tree
- **Main characteristics:** Aromatic pulp, shade canopy, seasonal abundance

## 04 — Papaya / *Carica papaya*

Template: **B**  
Left count: **416**  
Right count: **1782**  
Note count: **128**

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Papaya grows quickly and visibly, with a slender soft trunk, large hand-shaped leaves and fruit held close beneath the crown. In Bali it belongs to gardens, kitchens and small farms: ripe fruit is eaten fresh for sweetness, while green papaya is treated more like a vegetable, sliced or cooked for texture and mild flavour. Its upright form is easy to read, with leaf, flower and fruit gathered on one living column.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

Carica papaya is one of the fastest fruiting plants of the tropical garden, often producing within the first year in warm, fertile conditions. Its form is simple but expressive: a single soft stem marked by leaf scars, a crown of broad palmate leaves, and smooth fruits clustered directly along the upper trunk. In Bali, papaya is valued because it is immediate and versatile. Ripe fruit is eaten at breakfast, blended into juices, served with lime, or offered as a cooling market fruit. Green papaya has another role: firm, pale and only lightly sweet, it can be shredded, boiled or cooked in vegetable dishes, giving body without dominating the spice base. Papaya also contains papain, a proteolytic enzyme associated with the latex of the plant and widely known for tenderizing meat and supporting industrial food uses. Leaves and seeds appear in many tropical traditional practices, but they should be treated with care rather than romantic exaggeration; in everyday Bali, the fruit is the main useful part. Ecologically, papaya is a pioneer of disturbed garden edges. It likes sun, drains quickly after rain, and fits into small spaces where larger trees would be too heavy. Birds and bats may visit ripe fruit, while fallen fruit returns easily to the soil. The plant’s weakness is also part of its character: stems are soft, life is relatively short, and old plants are often replaced by seedlings or volunteers. Papaya is therefore a fruit of movement rather than permanence. It rises, fruits, leans, declines and is renewed. In a field guide, it should be read as the quick generous plant of the compound, bridging household food, practical enzyme chemistry and the informal ecology of gardens. Its open crown and exposed fruit make it easy to recognize in a Bali compound.

### NOTE ECOLOGICHE

Papaya gives ripe fruit, green vegetable flesh and papain-rich latex, thriving in sunny Bali gardens and disturbed garden edges.

### CARD

- **Season:** Year-round
- **Location:** Lowland and mid-altitude gardens
- **Tree height:** 3–10 m
- **Yield:** 30–100 fruits per plant per year
- **Main characteristics:** Vitamin-rich pulp, green fruit use, papain enzyme

## 09 — Durian / *Durio zibethinus*

Template: **B**  
Left count: **410**  
Right count: **1767**  
Note count: **127**

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Durian is not a discreet fruit. When it arrives in Bali’s markets, its presence is announced by size, spines and scent before anyone cuts it open. The tree is large and serious, bearing heavy fruits from branches and older limbs. Loved or refused, durian turns eating into conversation, judgment and shared experience. The fruit feels geological and handmade at once, shaped by thorn, soil and patient farming.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

Durio zibethinus grows as a tall tropical tree with glossy leaves and large flowers that open mainly in the evening. In many parts of Southeast Asia the flowers are associated with nocturnal pollinators, especially bats, and the fruit develops as a heavy spiny capsule containing rich edible arils around large seeds. In Bali, durian is mostly encountered as a seasonal market event: whole fruits stacked near roadsides, sellers tapping and smelling them, customers choosing by aroma, weight and trust. The flesh can be pale yellow to deep gold, custard-like, sweet, savoury, sometimes bitter, and always unmistakable. It is eaten fresh, added to sweets, made into pancakes or ice creams, and used in desserts where its perfume remains dominant. The seeds, after cooking, are edible in some regional practices, while the rind can be dried or burned as fuel in other parts of Southeast Asia. Traditional medicinal references exist, but here they remain ethnobotanical context, not home advice. Ecologically, durian belongs to humid lowland and foothill orchards rather than exposed coastal land. A mature tree creates shade, flowers at night, and produces fruit heavy enough to shape human behaviour: people wait, listen, collect and sell. The fallen fruit is part of the drama, because ripeness is often associated with natural drop. In Bali, durian’s importance is not only nutritional; it is social. Eating it requires presence. One does not quietly add durian to the background of a meal. Its smell enters the air, its texture divides opinion, and its season gathers people around a table or roadside stall. In visual terms, durian gives the fruit chapter a strong sculptural counterpoint: a dangerous-looking husk around a soft interior. It is also night habitat.

### NOTE ECOLOGICHE

Durian offers rich seasonal flesh and dramatic market culture, while humid-orchard trees feed night ecology and deep shade too.

### CARD

- **Season:** December–March
- **Location:** Lowland and foothill orchards
- **Tree height:** 25–50 m
- **Yield:** 50–150 fruits per tree
- **Main characteristics:** Spiny husk, custard flesh, powerful aroma

## 10 — Salak / *Salacca zalacca*

Template: **B**  
Left count: **409**  
Right count: **1776**  
Note count: **128**

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Salak is one of Bali’s most place-specific fruits, closely associated with Karangasem and the volcanic eastern slopes. The palm is low, spiny and clustered, hiding fruit near the base rather than displaying it in a crown. Under the scaly skin, the crisp pale segments taste sweet, sharp and slightly dry. Its presence belongs to dry eastern slopes, where patience, thorn and volcanic soil shape flavour. Rare.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

Salacca zalacca is a clustering palm with stiff spiny fronds and fruit borne in compact bunches close to the ground. This growth habit makes harvesting physical and careful: the fruit is not hanging openly from a tree, but protected inside a thorny architecture of leaves. Bali’s salak is especially linked to the Sibetan and Karangasem area, where salak agroforestry has become internationally recognized as an important agricultural heritage system. There, the palm is grown with other crops in a landscape shaped by volcanic soil, limited water and local knowledge. The fruit is called snake fruit because of its reddish-brown scale-like skin. Once peeled, it breaks into firm lobes with a texture somewhere between apple and nut, carrying sweetness, acidity and a gentle astringency. Salak is eaten fresh, but it can also be processed into sweets, syrup, chips, fermented products or local souvenirs. Its cultural value comes from more than taste: it represents a dryland farming intelligence adapted to Bali’s east, where mixed cultivation helps conserve water, maintain biodiversity and support village livelihoods. The palm itself also tells a different botanical story from the island’s taller fruit trees. It is defensive, shaded, ground-hugging and architectural. Fruit appears near the base, close to soil and leaf litter, and the plant forms dense clumps rather than open crowns. In a Bali field guide, salak deserves a special tone because it is not simply a tropical fruit in the market; it is a landscape fruit, tied to place, labour and heritage. Its scaly skin feels almost reptilian, but the flavour is bright, compact and memorable. Few fruits connect Bali’s geology, farming culture and tactile experience so directly. It adds dryland skill to the section.

### NOTE ECOLOGICHE

Salak links crisp snake-skinned fruit with Karangasem agroforestry, dryland resilience and Bali’s volcanic eastern slopes today.

### CARD

- **Season:** Year-round, peak June–August
- **Location:** Karangasem volcanic slopes and dryland gardens
- **Tree height:** 2–6 m cluster palm
- **Yield:** 50–200 fruits per cluster
- **Main characteristics:** Scaly skin, crisp flesh, agroforestry heritage

## 11 — Jackfruit / *Artocarpus heterophyllus*

Left count: **413**

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Jackfruit is abundance made visible: huge green fruits swelling from the trunk and older branches of a broad tropical tree. In Bali it is useful at two very different moments. Young jackfruit becomes a vegetable for savoury cooking, while ripe fruit breaks into golden bulbs that are sweet, aromatic and generous. The tree feels communal, because one fruit can become a meal, dessert, or reason to gather. Shared.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

Artocarpus heterophyllus is one of the great multipurpose trees of the tropical lowlands. Its massive fruits grow directly from the trunk and main branches, a habit known as cauliflory, which makes the tree instantly recognizable even from a distance. In Bali and the wider Indonesian kitchen, young jackfruit, nangka muda, is treated more like a vegetable than a fruit. It can be boiled, chopped and mixed with spices, coconut and sometimes meat in dishes such as lawar nangka, where its fibrous texture carries flavour and gives substance. In other Indonesian regions, young jackfruit is famously slow-cooked into gudeg, showing how the same plant travels through regional food cultures. When ripe, the fruit changes character completely. The golden arils become sweet, fragrant and chewy, eaten fresh or used in sweets, snacks and desserts. The seeds can be boiled or roasted after proper cooking. Leaves and young shoots are used in some tropical food and fodder traditions, while the wood is valued in parts of Asia for its warm colour and workability. In Bali, however, the tree is most present as a provider of shade, heavy fruit and kitchen versatility. Ecologically, jackfruit fits well into mixed gardens because one mature tree offers canopy, organic litter, animal food and human food in large quantity. Its scale matters: a single fruit can feed many people, and harvesting often requires planning rather than casual picking. The tree therefore has a social quality. It invites sharing, cutting, dividing and cooking. In an illustrated spread, jackfruit should feel monumental but domestic: a large tree whose fruit is both vegetable and dessert, field biology and household generosity in one form. Sticky latex and rough rind make preparation slow, but social.

### NOTE ECOLOGICHE

Jackfruit gives shade, young vegetable flesh and ripe sweet bulbs, making one tree a generous source for Bali kitchens and sharing.

### CARD

- **Season:** April–September
- **Location:** Lowland gardens and cultivated areas
- **Tree height:** 10–20 m
- **Yield:** 20–80 fruits per tree
- **Main characteristics:** Huge trunk-borne fruit, young vegetable use

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Soursop is visually rough but internally delicate. Its green skin carries soft spines, while the white flesh inside is fragrant, fibrous and sharply sweet. In Bali it appears in humid gardens and juice stalls, where the pulp is blended into cooling drinks, desserts and simple preparations for hot afternoons. Its charm is contrast: a prickly outside, a gentle white interior, and a flavour made for shade and ice.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

Annona muricata is a small tropical tree with glossy leaves, unusual thick-petalled flowers and large irregular fruits. The fruit’s surface is green and softly spined, but the interior is pale, aromatic and juicy, with a flavour often described as a meeting of pineapple, strawberry and citrus acidity. In Bali, soursop is mainly appreciated as a fresh pulp for juice, smoothies, ice cream and cold desserts. It is not a fruit of clean slices; it is scooped, strained, blended and softened into drinks. The seeds are not eaten. Young fruits are cooked as vegetables in some Indonesian and tropical traditions, but the common Balinese encounter is the ripe fruit transformed into a cooling drink. Leaves of soursop have a wide reputation in traditional medicine across the tropics, and they are often discussed in relation to teas and herbal preparations. Because scientific evidence and safety questions remain complex, this book should not present them as treatment; it is enough to note that the plant carries strong ethnobotanical interest. Ecologically, soursop prefers warm humid conditions and fits into lowland gardens, edges and small farms. Its modest size makes it more domestic than monumental, suitable for house compounds where fruit can be collected without climbing. The tree also has an understated beauty: elongated leaves, downward flowers and fruit that seems both primitive and tender. In a field-guide spread, soursop can be used to show contrast: hard-looking exterior, soft edible center; medicinal reputation, but everyday use as juice; small tree, large fruit. In Bali’s fruit culture, it is less ceremonial than coconut and less iconic than banana, yet it is deeply useful in the informal economy of refreshments, home gardens and market stalls.

### NOTE ECOLOGICHE

Soursop offers cooling aromatic pulp for juices, while its modest tree suits Bali’s humid lowland gardens and small farms well.

### CARD

- **Season:** June–September
- **Location:** Humid lowland gardens and farms
- **Tree height:** 5–8 m
- **Yield:** 20–50 fruits per tree

## 13 — Starfruit / *Averrhoa carambola*

Left count: **415**

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Starfruit is one of the most graphic fruits in the tropical garden. Hanging from a modest tree, the ribbed yellow fruit seems simple until it is cut across, when each slice becomes a clean star. In Bali it is valued for freshness: crisp, watery, lightly acidic and useful in juice, garnish and market fruit plates. It turns botany into geometry, making even a simple garden slice feel precise and memorable. Always.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

Averrhoa carambola is a small evergreen tree with compound leaves, delicate pinkish flowers and distinctive ribbed fruit. The plant has a quiet elegance: its leaflets may fold in response to light or touch, while fruits hang like translucent lanterns along branches and older wood. In Bali, starfruit is familiar as belimbing, eaten fresh when crisp and juicy or pressed into refreshing drinks. The fruit can be sweet or tart depending on variety and maturity, making it useful both as a snack and as a bright acidic accent. Its five ridges are not only decorative; they define the fruit’s identity, giving the famous star shape when sliced crosswise. In home gardens, starfruit has the advantage of being medium-sized and productive without dominating the compound. It flowers and fruits in repeated cycles under warm conditions, so a tree may carry different stages at once. Leaves, roots and fruit have recorded traditional uses in parts of Asia, especially in household herbal practice, but caution is important: starfruit is known to be unsafe for people with kidney disease because of compounds that can accumulate. For a public field guide, it is better to describe its culinary freshness rather than promote medicinal use. Ecologically, the flowers attract small insects, while fallen fruit may feed birds or animals. The tree’s value is visual as much as edible: it gives light shade, fine foliage and fruit with a geometry that children and cooks both recognize. In Bali’s fruit chapter, starfruit offers a pause from heavy tropical abundance. It is not massive like jackfruit or overwhelming like durian; it is crisp, lucid and almost architectural. Its charm lies in how a modest garden fruit becomes, with a single cut, a botanical diagram of a star.

### NOTE ECOLOGICHE

Starfruit brings crisp watery fruit, small-tree shade and repeat flowering, adding clear geometry to Bali’s garden fruit culture.

### CARD

- **Season:** Year-round, rainy-season peaks
- **Location:** Lowland and garden environments
- **Tree height:** 5–12 m

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Guava is an ordinary garden fruit with extraordinary fragrance. The tree is resilient, compact and useful, with peeling bark, textured leaves and fruits that may hold white or pink flesh. In Bali it is eaten fresh, juiced, sprinkled with seasoning, or left for birds when too ripe to collect. Its value is direct: easy to grow, easy to share, and fragrant before it is even seen in the garden. It stays close.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

Psidium guajava is a small tropical tree with smooth peeling bark, opposite aromatic leaves and round to pear-shaped fruits. Its strength is adaptability: guava grows in lowland gardens, dry edges, village compounds and small farms with less ceremony than many prized tropical fruits. The fruit may be green, yellow, white-fleshed or pink-fleshed depending on variety, and its scent often announces ripeness before colour does. In Bali, guava is eaten fresh, cut with salt or chili-sugar mixtures, blended into juice, or used in simple preserves. The seeds form a firm central mass, while the flesh ranges from crisp and pale to soft, pink and highly perfumed. Guava leaves are among the better-known tropical leaves in household herbal traditions, especially in relation to digestion, and modern reviews discuss their antioxidant and antimicrobial compounds. Ecologically, guava can be both useful and vigorous. Birds and bats are attracted to ripe fruit, seeds disperse easily, and seedlings may appear without being planted. In a managed Bali garden this is welcome; in sensitive habitats, any easily dispersed fruit tree must be watched. The tree contributes shade, flowers for insects, and leaf litter for the soil. Its bark, with soft grey and copper tones, gives it a visual identity even when fruit is absent. Guava’s appeal is its nearness to everyday life. It does not require a special season of celebration; it is simply there, often beside a house or path, offering fruit that can be eaten immediately from the hand. In the field guide, guava should read as practical, fragrant and quietly generous: a small tree carrying food, leaf knowledge and a strong memory of tropical gardens. The tree’s resilience and scent make it practical in Bali compounds and market edges.

### NOTE ECOLOGICHE

Guava gives fragrant fruit, insect flowers and useful leaves, thriving as a resilient small tree in Bali’s home gardens and farms.

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Passion fruit brings a different rhythm to Bali’s fruit gardens: it climbs rather than stands, fastening itself to trellises, fences and garden edges with curling tendrils. The vine is valued for its aromatic pulp, but its flower is equally remarkable, with an intricate crown of filaments that feels almost architectural. In cooler upland gardens and cultivated plots, the fruit ripens into purple or yellow spheres filled with tart, fragrant juice and black seeds. It is used fresh, spooned over fruit, mixed into drinks and desserts, and appreciated for the bright acidity it brings to tropical sweetness. A single vine can turn a simple support into shade, flower, scent and fruit. It feels light, precise and unexpectedly intense. It deserves an open, vertical page.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

Passion fruit, Passiflora edulis, is a vigorous climbing vine with three-lobed leaves, tendrils and structured flowers. Each flower opens with pale petals and a corona of fine filaments surrounding the central reproductive column, a form that invites close observation rather than casual viewing. Pollination is often carried out by bees, and successful fruit set depends on their movement through the garden. The fruit develops as a rounded berry with a firm outer rind and an interior of golden-orange pulp around many seeds. In Bali it is less dominant than coconut or banana, but it appears in markets, home gardens and upland growing areas where cooler air and supports allow the vine to perform well. Its role is mostly culinary: the pulp is stirred into juices, syrups, desserts, fruit bowls and sauces, where its sharp perfume balances cream, sugar and richer tropical fruits. The rind is not normally eaten fresh, though passion fruit by-products are used elsewhere for pectin, jam texture and processed foods. Ecologically, the vine shows how vertical space can become productive. It shades a fence, softens a wall, attracts pollinators, and produces fruit without needing the footprint of a tree. It can also become vigorous, so cultivated plants are usually trained, pruned and supported. For the field guide, passion fruit is important because it joins edible use with botanical complexity: a vine of tendrils and leaves, a flower like a small natural machine, and a fruit whose scent is stronger than its size suggests. In Bali’s warmer lowlands it may need care, but in cooler gardens it rewards attention with a vivid, refreshing harvest. Its value is not abundance alone, but the contrast between delicate flower geometry and juice that tastes bright and immediate.

### NOTE ECOLOGICHE

Passion fruit links ornate flowers, pollinating bees and tart aromatic pulp, making trellises productive in Bali’s upland gardens.

## 16 — Water Apple / *Syzygium aqueum*

Template: **B**  
Left count: **416**  
Right count: **1783**  
Note count: **131**

### LEFT MAIN TEXT

Water apple, or jambu air, is a fruit of refreshment rather than heaviness. Bell-shaped, crisp and full of water, it is eaten fresh in the heat, often before it ever reaches a formal recipe. In Bali’s gardens it brings glossy leaves, pale flowers and light fruit that suit the domestic landscape. In the heat, its pale crunch can feel more useful than richness or perfume, almost like edible water. It cools the day.

### RIGHT MAIN TEXT

Syzygium aqueum is a small to medium tree in the myrtle family, bearing glossy leaves, brush-like flowers and bell-shaped fruit with thin waxy skin. The fruit may be white, pale pink or red depending on variety, but its character remains similar: crisp, watery, lightly sweet and cooling. In Bali it is commonly known through the broader world of jambu fruits, eaten fresh as a snack, cut into fruit plates, or paired with spicy-sweet seasoning in informal market eating. It is not a dense fruit; it is almost a drink held in a delicate structure. This makes it especially welcome in hot lowland gardens, where it can be picked and eaten quickly. The flowers are also visually interesting, with many fine stamens forming soft pale clusters that attract insects. Leaves and bark have recorded traditional uses in parts of Southeast Asia, and recent studies discuss bioactive compounds in water apple leaves, but the Balinese everyday value remains simple: shade, fruit, freshness and garden beauty. Ecologically, the tree likes humid tropical conditions and fits well in village compounds, villa gardens and small orchards. Its fruits are easily bruised compared with hard-rinded tropical fruits, so they belong more to local consumption than long-distance shipping. That local quality is part of their charm. Water apple teaches a different category of fruit appreciation: not richness, not perfume, not sugar, but texture and coolness. A good fruit snaps softly under the teeth and releases water more than pulp. In the field guide, it should feel light, almost translucent, with the plant’s glossy leaves and airy flower clusters supporting the freshness of the fruit.
